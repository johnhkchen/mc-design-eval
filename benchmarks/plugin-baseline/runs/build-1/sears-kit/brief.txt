The mail-order house kit: eight family houses after the Sears, Roebuck "Modern Homes" catalog (1908-1940), the pre-cut kit houses shipped by rail and raised by their owners in streetcar suburbs and small towns across America. Each module is ONE catalog model, recognisable from the street the way the catalog's front-view drawing is: its roof form, porch and window rhythm carry the identity. These join a public catalog of buildings for a Minecraft 1.21.1 survival server (Create mod) and will be set along a new suburban street on a survival landscape, so they must read as a street that belongs together, built in the same years, yet each house distinct at a glance. Use only block ids that exist in Minecraft 1.21.1 (no pale oak, no 1.21.4+ blocks).

Build it as ONE sample row of modules on a fixed plot grid, so each plot can be cut out as its own module file:
THE GRID: a street runs along x at z 0..4: a 3-wide gravel or path-block road (z 0..2) and a 2-wide smooth stone sidewalk (z 3..4); it stands for the town's street. Plots are 16 wide (x) and 20 deep (z 5..24); plot i covers x = 16*i .. 16*i+15. Each house faces the street (north), set back behind a front lawn, its front walk running from the sidewalk to the main door. No front fence (a lawn running to the sidewalk, as in the catalog drawings); a low hedge or picket fence only along the back and sides if any, the same on every plot. Nothing a module builds may leave its plot. 8 plots in a row (128 wide):
 plot 0: The Magnolia (Georgian / Colonial Revival, the catalog's grandest house): two and a half storeys, symmetrical five-bay front, a full-height portico of tall white columns, a hipped or side-gabled roof with dormers, a balcony over the front door, white clapboard
 plot 1: The Alhambra (Mission Revival): stucco walls, curved shaped parapet gables, an arcaded front porch with round arches, a low red-tiled look to the roof edges, two storeys
 plot 2: The Vallonia (Craftsman bungalow, the most-built Sears house): one and a half storeys, a side-gabled roof that sweeps down over a full-width front porch, a wide shed or gabled dormer, tapered porch posts on brick or stone piers, exposed rafter tails and knee braces
 plot 3: The Puritan (Dutch Colonial): a gambrel roof with a long shed dormer, a small pedimented entry porch with columns, shingle or clapboard, two storeys under the gambrel
 plot 4: The Cornell (American Foursquare): a two-storey cube, a low hipped roof with a hipped dormer on the front, a full-width one-storey front porch with square columns, a four-rooms-up, four-rooms-down plan
 plot 5: The Stanford / Cape Cod (1931): one and a half storeys, steep side-gabled roof with two gabled dormers, a centred door with shutters at the windows, a central chimney, white clapboard or shingle
 plot 6: The Winona (a modest, much-built cottage): one storey, front-gabled, a small porch under the gable, simple and cheap but cared for: the starter home of the row
 plot 7: a Craftsman two-storey house to close the row: front-gabled with a broad porch on stone piers, wide eaves with brackets, a mix of shingle above and clapboard below
Materials across the row: painted clapboard (white, cream, pale yellow, sage, grey), wood shingle, brick, stucco, stone porch piers; roofs in dark shingle, red, green or grey; each house its own colour scheme, all of the same era so they sit together on one street. Windows in real rhythm (double-hung pairs and triples, never hole-punched single panes), trim around them, shutters where the style has them.
Each house sleeps a villager family: 3 to 4 beds, every bed whole, its head against a solid wall (never a window, a door or nothing), two clear blocks above it, reachable from the door; a living room, a kitchen with a stove, a table. Furnished like the catalog's floor plans: parlour, dining room, kitchen, bedrooms upstairs.
THE WAY IN, from the sidewalk to every main door, is held to an accessibility standard: half steps only (bottom slabs: slab, slab, slab), at most 3 in a row then a level landing 2 long, 2 wide or more, level for the last cell before the door; never a stoop of stairs, never a full-block step. Keep the ground floor low: 1 above the lawn at most (a 2-cell slab ramp), so the front lawn has room for it.
Stairs inside a player walks to the top without jumping (flight(): the top step flush with the upper floor). Chimneys as the owner wants them: the fire burns at the BOTTOM in a hearth (campfire with hay hidden in the floor), the smoke rises up a 1x1 air flue walled all round to an open mouth 1-2 courses above the roof; the chimney is SET INTO THE WALL (an exterior chimney: the fire just outside the wall line, the wall cell its mouth, the flue outside; or in a partition wall), never standing in a room; the stack is its own masonry running solid through the roof with the roof stopping against it, constant width, no crown; each chimney fits INSIDE the plot (chimney(g, { wall, into, top, ... })). Lit so no mob spawns.
